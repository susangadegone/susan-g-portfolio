01 · The Lens

Empathy debt is the cumulative cost of choosing speed, metrics, or automation over actually understanding the people you serve.

It builds up quietly. A feature ships before the team talks to a real user. A customer service flow gets automated before anyone listens to what people are actually calling about. Tone gets approved in a brand guide before a single person in crisis sees it.

The bill comes due later. As churn. As one star reviews nobody reads carefully. As a person who needed help, didn't get it, and tells nobody why.

I learned to spot empathy debt because I spent years in the room. Supporting transition-age youth, foster families, and partners across clinical, school, residential, and legal systems. I've also lived through versions of what the young people I worked with were facing. The framework is the residue of both.

The empathy gap is not a UX problem. It is the cost of designing without proximity to the people you claim to serve.

02 · The Work

A few of the things I've built. Each one was a way of asking: what would this look like if the person using it was treated like a whole human?

i.
Solo iOS · in progress
Product Design Accessibility

Cozy

Most household task apps are productivity tools in pastel clothing. They run on streaks and shame. Cozy is for one person, in their own home, on their own rhythm.

The interaction model is pen and paper. You check a chore off. You add one in two taps. No badges yelling at you for missing a day. The point of Cozy is to spend more time on the chore and less time fighting the app.

I stripped multi user functionality from v1 to test the solo concept first. Seeded a starter chore library. Made design choices that keep the app from drifting into the generic AI look that's everywhere right now.

What I did: Product direction, design system, copy, accessibility decisions, SwiftUI build with engineer support, Supabase backend.

ii.
Cultural app · shipped
Content Community Design

India Festivals

A calendar built by someone who grew up around the celebrations and wanted a way to learn them more deeply alongside family.

Festival calendars from outsiders flatten what they document. They miss the regional differences, the family rituals, the reason behind a date. This one is for the diaspora user who wants context, not just a date on a screen.

The design reads as cultural fluency, not exoticism. Translation in Hindi handled directly instead of routed through generic localization. The roadmap includes more festivals over time, and eventually festivals from other cultures.

What I did: Content strategy, translation review, information architecture, visual design.

iii.
Wellness tool · coming soon

Cal Mood Tracker

If you keep a calendar for the rest of your life, why not keep one for your wellness?

Cal is a mood and wellness tracker built around the rhythms you already have, not around streaks that punish you for being human. Logging is fast. Patterns surface gently. The copy reads like it was written by someone who has had a hard day.

03 · Origin

Edgewood Center is where I learned how to listen.

For five plus years I was Lead Peer Mentor at Edgewood Center for Children and Families. I managed a team of seven to eight peer partners. We served transition-age youth, foster families, and partners across clinical, school, residential, legal, and administrative systems.

The work was meaningful, demanding, and real. We were a team that cared about showing up for young adults in the community. The communication had to be clear because someone's day depended on it.

Most product people I meet have read a book about empathy. I've talked to people who lost loved ones to suicide. To people who couldn't make sense of why they were in foster care. To people who didn't want to be on drugs but were, because the drugs were stronger than the support they had. I don't share that for sympathy. I share it because it is the lens I bring to every product question, every conversation, every interaction.
