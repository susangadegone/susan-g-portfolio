01What I do

Why frontline work prepares me for wellness programs.

01

Plan events around what people need

Every year I planned Back to School, Hoodie Haul and Thanksgiving Dinner for the center. The biggest grew from about 50 people the first year I ran it to about 80. Each one ran on $500 to $2,000, stretched with donations from local businesses, community organizations and individual donors, and I handled the outreach myself. After each event I looked at what fell flat and changed it the next year. All three grew.

02

Keep a program going when the format changes

When COVID closed our doors, our team moved everything to deliveries and online groups. I helped work out how the new process would run. Then I did a lot of it. I ran deliveries, led online sessions and texted youth who had gone quiet. Our reach grew. In about a year, more than 125 young people used our services, more than in a typical year in person, and most of our regulars stayed with us.

What carries overPrograms sometimes have to change format fast. I moved one from in person to delivery and online while people still needed it.

03

Train people and report what's working

As lead peer partner I trained and onboarded 5+ new peer staff, set the schedule and ran the center during my shifts. I was the person the team came to first.

I wrote the data reports our executive team used to make decisions, pulling from spreadsheets, surveys and our records system. That same program data went into a grant that was funded for our COVID response and health and wellness events.

Feedback changed what we ran. Youth loved our online game night while the center was closed. When we reopened, the ones who couldn't get to the center still wanted in, so we ran game night in person and online at the same time.

What carries overI can turn what happens on the ground into something leadership can act on.

04

Look after the people doing the work

Peer support is draining work, and burnout is common. After hard days I debriefed with the peer staff. I ran regular check-ins on how people were actually doing, not just what they had done that week. And I planned events just for the team.

What carries overThis is employee wellness on a small scale, in a job where people burn out fast.

05

Design wellness apps

Since 2023 I've been building wellness apps and learning product through programs like SheBuilds. This year I built Take Care, for families sharing the care of someone with Parkinson's, and then Roam. In 2024, Stanford's AI for Good program picked me as 1 of 30 from 230 applicants. There I came up with MindMate and led our team, and we made the top 8 that pitched to investors. The same year I won a Neolth Innovation Challenge All Star Award for a mental health toolkit concept. It became the starting point for Roam.

Roam

E / 2026 / prototype

Roam is a mental health resource finder for San Francisco and the East Bay. It helps you find free and low-cost places to go. When going somewhere isn't realistic, it offers coping skills you can try right now, based on how you're feeling today. Crisis help is always one tap away.

How I'm building Roam
The problem
Resource lists are long, and a hard day is the wrong time to sort through one.
My decisions
Focus on free and low-cost places. Personalize to how someone feels today. Offer a coping skill when going somewhere isn't realistic. Keep crisis help one tap away.
Where the list comes from
Public directories. Before anyone relies on it, I plan to confirm each listing myself, including cost, hours and whether it is still open. I haven't done that yet.
What I'd measure first
Whether people actually go to a place Roam suggests. For coping skills, how someone feels before and after.
Where it stands
A working prototype. Nobody outside the build has used it yet.
