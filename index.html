About

I know your user before they open the app.

Technology and AI are becoming mental health advocates in their own right. They're reaching people the care system never could. I want to be the person who makes sure those tools are actually ready for the people using them — not just ready to ship.

I spent years in behavioral health sitting with young people through some of the hardest moments of their lives. That gave me something most product people don't have: I know what it looks like when someone is in crisis and the tool they're reaching for isn't working. You can't learn that from a persona.

That's why I care about trust and safety. Not as a policy checkbox, but as the real question of who gets hurt if this goes wrong. I've seen what happens when systems fail the people who needed them most. I know what “safe” means to someone in crisis, not just what it means on paper.

I've watched technology promise real care and give people something hollow instead. I've also seen what happens when someone in the room asks who this could hurt before it launches, not after. That's the difference between a tool that actually helps and one that just says it does. I want to be that person in the room.

I bring trauma-informed thinking into product decisions as a practical lens, not a philosophy. It shapes how I think about retention mechanics, crisis features, notification ethics, and what trust actually means when your user is struggling. I've built wellness and productivity apps from scratch using Cursor, Claude, Google AI Studio, Replit, and Vercel, and I write about digital health product design on Substack.

If you're building something that helps people get care they wouldn't otherwise have access to, and you want someone asking who this could hurt before it ships, I want to talk.

The Translation

Product leaders keep asking how mental health experience becomes product instinct. Here's exactly what it looks like in the room.

Not hypotheticals. This is what I'd actually be doing on your team, in your meetings, on your roadmap.

In mental health work

Decided when a safety concern had to go up the chain and when it didn't, knowing a wrong call in either direction could hurt someone.

↓ At your company

When your trust and safety team debates where the line is for self-harm content, I've made that call with a real person in front of me. I help write severity policy that holds up at 2am, not just in the review meeting.

In mental health work

Explained confidentiality and its limits to scared teenagers before they'd tell me anything, and lived by hard rules about what I could share, with whom, and when.

↓ At your company

When the growth team wants to use mood data for personalization, I know the difference between consent someone understood and consent someone clicked through. That difference is your users' trust.

In mental health work

Practiced trauma-informed care, where you never use shame or fear as a lever, because the person in front of you has had enough of both.

↓ At your company

Streaks, red badges, “we miss you” notifications — I can tell you which mechanics motivate your user and which punish them for having a bad week. I built Cozy, a chore app, by stripping the guilt out.

In mental health work

Trained and led a team of peer mentors, teaching people to make consistent calls in ambiguous, high-stakes situations.

↓ At your company

Scaling judgment is the hardest problem in trust and safety. I've done it the analog way: turning my own instincts into guidance a new mentor could follow when I wasn't in the room. That's what good policy is.

In mental health work

Read when someone in crisis was about to disengage, often before they said a word, because I'd learned exactly what that looked like.

↓ At your company

In a churn review, I don't stop at the drop-off chart. I pull real user sessions from the week before they left and tell you who left because of a bug, and who left because the product made them feel stupid or judged.

In mental health work

Sat in rooms where clinicians, schools, and families each had a different read on the same kid, and my job was getting the room to one decision.

↓ At your company

When engineering wants speed, design wants polish, and support is fielding angry tickets, I find what's actually true about the user underneath each argument and turn three positions into one decision.

In mental health work

Wrote safety plans a scared, overwhelmed person had to get right on the first read, with no one there to explain it to them.

↓ At your company

When onboarding drop-off spikes, I don't start with A/B tests on button copy. I read what the screen assumes the person already knows, and rewrite it for someone reading it once, stressed, on a phone, at 11pm.

In mental health work

Watched, in real time, where a care system's good intentions turned into a person falling through the cracks, and had to notice fast enough to catch them.

↓ At your company

When your team debates whether an AI feature should hand off to a human, I've sat with someone in the moment that needed a human being. I can tell you where that line is and what it costs to get it wrong.
