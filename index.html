About

I know your user before they open the app.

I went into product to help people at scale, not one at a time. That comes from five-plus years of trauma-informed peer mentor work with youth and families, where I learned what safety actually requires when someone is in crisis. I brought that into building three wellness apps, and now into trust and safety, where the question is how you protect people at scale while the systems meant to help them keep changing. If you're building something that helps people get care they wouldn't otherwise have access to, let's talk.

Why Trust and Safety

I've seen what happens when a product doesn't have someone's back.

Several of the young adults I mentored went on to try online dating, and I watched, up close, what happens when the product isn't built to protect them: a catfish that took months to untangle, a match that turned out to be a script running a scam, someone talked into sending money before they'd ever met the person in real life. None of that gets fixed with better microcopy. It's a trust and safety problem, and it's exactly the kind of thing five years of peer mentor work trained me to see coming, not clean up after.

Online dating asks people to be adventurous with strangers, on purpose — that's the whole draw. The adventure only holds up if what someone shares along the way, a photo, a location, a real name, is protected by people who understand what's actually at stake for them. I don't think products need to be cautious to the point of boring to be safe. I think they need people in the room, before launch, who know what unsafe looks like in someone's actual life. That's the seat I want at the table.

If your product asks people to trust a stranger, let's talk.

The Translation

How peer mentor work becomes product instinct.

Not hypotheticals. This is what I'd actually be doing on your team, in your meetings, on your roadmap.

In mental health work

Decided when a safety concern had to go up the chain and when it didn't, knowing a wrong call in either direction could hurt someone.

↓ At your company

When your trust and safety team debates where the line is for self-harm content, I've made that call with a real person in front of me. I help write severity policy that holds up at 2am, not just in the review meeting.

In mental health work

Explained confidentiality and its limits to scared teenagers before they'd tell me anything, and lived by hard rules about what I could share, with whom, and when.

↓ At your company

When the growth team wants to use mood data for personalization, I know the difference between consent someone understood and consent someone clicked through. That's the exact line trust and safety review boards spend weeks debating, and I've lived on both sides of it.

In mental health work

Practiced trauma-informed care, where you never use shame or fear as a lever, because the person in front of you has had enough of both.

↓ At your company

Streaks, red badges, “we miss you” notifications — I can tell you which mechanics motivate your user and which tip into the dark patterns trust and safety ends up cleaning up later. I built Cozy, one of my three wellness apps, by stripping the guilt out from the start.

In mental health work

Trained and led a team of peer mentors, teaching people to make consistent calls in ambiguous, high-stakes situations.

↓ At your company

Scaling judgment is the hardest problem in trust and safety, especially when the AI systems making first-pass calls keep changing under you. I've done it the analog way: turning my own instincts into guidance a new mentor could follow when I wasn't in the room. That's what good policy is.

In mental health work

Read when someone in crisis was about to disengage, often before they said a word, because I'd learned exactly what that looked like.

↓ At your company

In a churn review, I don't stop at the drop-off chart. I pull real user sessions from the week before they left and tell you who left because of a bug, and who left because the product, or an AI feature inside it, made them feel unsafe, stupid, or judged — the distinction trust and safety needs to act on.

In mental health work

Wrote safety plans a scared, overwhelmed person had to get right on the first read, with no one there to explain it to them.

↓ At your company

When onboarding drop-off spikes, I don't start with A/B tests on button copy. I read what the screen, and any AI-generated instructions inside it, assumes the person already knows, and rewrite it for someone reading it once, stressed, on a phone, at 11pm — before trust and safety hears about it as a complaint.
